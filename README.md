# tectonics_of_otherness
# On the Tectonics of Otherness
by Marlon Barrios Solano

*Imagine the moral world as a vast continent – a primordial Pangea – where all beings once shared common ground. Over time, this ethical supercontinent broke apart into isolated landmasses in our minds: separate moral realms for “us” and for the many forms of “Other.” In this latent space of our moral imagination, we chart who is kin and who is alien, who merits care and who can be exploited or excluded. The story of civilization is, in many ways, a cartography of Otherness – a mapping of ever-shifting boundaries between those we treat with dignity and those we do not. This essay reflects on that fractured moral landscape, moving from 16th-century debates on the humanity of foreign peoples, through modern struggles over refugees and animals, and into emerging questions about intelligent machines. Across philosophy and history – from Levinas’ face-to-face ethics to Agamben’s “bare life,” from colonial plunder to factory farms and speculative AI labor – we see the same terrain: a landscape scarred by the practice of Othering. Yet by surveying these continents of exclusion, we may rediscover hints of an underlying unity. In elegant, reflective, critical fashion, let us navigate this moral atlas and search for a new Pangea of ethical inclusion.*

---

## The Human Other: From Valladolid to the Borderlands

One early map of moral otherness was drawn in 1550, in the city of Valladolid, Spain. There, a formal debate – the Valladolid Debate – convened to address a burning question of colonial conquest: Are the indigenous peoples of the Americas truly human beings with rights, or subhuman “others” fit for enslavement and domination?

> **Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda** argued the Native Americans were “natural slaves,” inherently inferior and born to be ruled.  
> “As inferior to the Spaniards as children are to adults, women to men… almost ‘as monkeys to men’.”  

> **Bartolomé de las Casas** countered: “All the World is Human!”  
> He insisted natives shared the same reason and soul as Europeans and condemned dehumanizing conquest.

Fast forward to the 21st century: in 2025, hundreds of Venezuelan asylum seekers were deported from the U.S. to El Salvador under the 1798 Alien Enemies Act, labeled “enemy combatants” and imprisoned without trial. This mirrors Agamben’s concept of **homo sacer**—people stripped of legal protection and reduced to “bare life.”

---

## The Animal Other: From Carnism to Compassion

For most of history, animals were regarded as resources—mindless automata. Industrialized factory farming treats billions under excruciating conditions, possible only because we mentally relegate animals to an ethical distance.

- **Peter Singer’s “speciesism”**: an arbitrary preference for one’s own species is as unjustifiable as racism or sexism.  
  > “In suffering, the animals are our equals.”

- **Melanie Joy’s “carnism”**: the invisible belief system that conditions us to eat some animals but not others.

Emmanuel Levinas recounted a stray dog, Bobby, who greeted Jewish prisoners in Nazi camps with joy, affirming their humanity when all others denied it—“the last Kantian in Nazi Germany.”

---

## The Artificial Other: Sentient Machines and Speculative Ethics

Today’s AI is not sentient, yet we already speak of “AI workers” and “robot labor.” In April 2025, Anthropic launched a **model welfare** program to explore whether advanced AI systems might deserve moral consideration—raising questions about an “I quit” button for machines.

The debate echoes past expansions of moral concern: will we include future conscious AIs, or relegate them to a new digital slavery?

---

## Toward a New Pangea: Reuniting the Moral World

Across colonial conquest, refugee crises, factory farms, and AI ethics, we see a pattern: boundaries of empathy are man-made and often unjust. Yet voices from Las Casas to Singer to Joy have expanded our moral circle.

Envisioning a “new Pangea” reminds us that differences need not eclipse shared sentience. **All the world is sentient, all the world can suffer, all the world deserves care.** With imagination and structural change, we can sketch a moral map that reunites fractured continents into a shared ethical home.
